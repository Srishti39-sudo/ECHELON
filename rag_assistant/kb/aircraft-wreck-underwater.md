## Three regimes, and which one applies

Aircraft wreckage on the seabed falls under a different authority depending on
what it is. The sources cover three cases and they must not be mixed.

A recent civil aircraft accident or incident in India is governed by the
Aircraft (Investigation of Accidents and Incidents) Rules, 2017.

An aircraft that has been under water for at least 100 years falls within the
definition of underwater cultural heritage, which expressly includes aircraft,
their cargo and other contents (UNESCO 2001 Convention, Article 1, paragraph
1(a)(ii)). In situ preservation is the first option (Article 2, paragraph 5).

A sunken military aircraft is a third case, described under military wrecks
below.

## Notification in India

Where an accident or incident occurs, the pilot-in-command or, if killed or
incapacitated, the owner, operator, hirer or other person on whose behalf he was
in command, or any relevant person, shall as soon as is reasonably practicable
and in any case not later than twenty-four hours after becoming aware of it:

- send notice to the Aircraft Accident Investigation Bureau and the Directorate
  General of Civil Aviation by the quickest means of communication available; and
- in the case of an accident or incident occurring in India, give information to
  the District Magistrate and the Officer-in-charge of the nearest Police
  Station, of the accident or incident and of the place where it occurred.

(Rule 4, sub-rule 1.)

## What the notification must contain

The notification shall be in plain language and contain as much of the following
as is readily available (Rule 4, sub-rule 2):

- the identifying abbreviation ACCID for accidents, INCID for incidents, SINCID
  for serious incidents
- manufacturer, model, nationality and registration marks, and serial number of
  the aircraft
- name of owner, operator and hirer, if any
- qualification of the pilot-in-command, and nationality of crew and passengers
- date and time, local time or UTC
- last point of departure and point of intended landing
- position of the aircraft with reference to some easily defined geographical
  point, and latitude and longitude
- number of crew and passengers aboard, killed and seriously injured, and others
  killed and seriously injured
- description of the occurrence and the extent of damage so far as is known
- physical characteristics of the area

## Do not disturb the wreckage

The aircraft and contents thereof shall not, except by a person under the
authority of the Director General, AAIB, be removed or otherwise interfered with
(Rule 7, sub-rule 2).

Three exceptions are stated. Persons authorised to conduct search and rescue may
remove or interfere with the aircraft so far as necessary to extricate persons or
animals dead or alive, to prevent destruction by fire or other cause, or to
prevent damage or obstruction to the public, to air navigation or to other
transport (Rule 7, sub-rule 2(a)).

If the aircraft is wrecked on water, the aircraft or any parts or contents may be
removed only to such extent as may be necessary for bringing it to a place of
safety, and only by persons authorised to conduct search and rescue operations
(Rule 7, sub-rule 2(b)).

Goods may be removed only under the supervision and with the concurrence of an
officer of the AAIB or a person authorised by the Director General, AAIB (Rule 7,
sub-rule 2(c)).

A survey vessel that has detected a contact is none of these.

## Custody of the evidence

In the case of an accident or serious incident, the Investigator-in-Charge shall
have unhampered access to the wreckage and all relevant material and
information, including flight recorders, and unrestricted control over it so
that a detailed examination can be made without delay (Rule 7, sub-rule 1).

The Aircraft Accident Investigation Bureau shall be the sole agency regarding
custody of the evidence collected during the investigation, including the
wreckage and recorders. Where another agency requires access, the Director
General, AAIB, may facilitate it while retaining custody (Rule 7, sub-rule 8).

Measures the Director General may authorise include protecting evidence,
preserving it by photographic or other means, maintaining safe custody against
further damage, access by unauthorised persons, pilfering and deterioration, and
preserving the aircraft for as long as the investigation requires (Rule 7,
sub-rule 3).

## Military aircraft

Sunken military craft owned by the United States remain the property of the
United States regardless of their location or the passage of time, and may not
be disturbed without permission from the U.S. Navy. The Naval History and
Heritage Command manages over 17,000 ship and aircraft wrecks worldwide. Such
craft often serve as war graves, carry unexploded ordnance, and contain oil or
other hazardous materials, and the policy is that they remain in place and
undisturbed.

An aircraft wreck may therefore be an accident site, a grave and an explosive
hazard at once.

## What these sources do not specify

These sources give NO standoff distance for aircraft wreckage on the seabed.
NOT SPECIFIED.

These sources contain NO salvage, recovery or lifting method, and nothing here
authorises an operator to attempt one.

The Indian rules quoted here are the civil aviation accident channel. They are
NOT the channel for suspected ordnance, and the presence of a police station in
the notification chain does not make them one.
