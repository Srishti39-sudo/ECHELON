## Definition

NATO defines a sea mine, also called a naval mine, as an explosive device laid
in the water with the intention of damaging or sinking ships or of deterring
shipping from entering an area.

## Categories of underwater explosive ordnance contamination

GICHD groups underwater contamination into sea mines, torpedoes, aerial
munitions, munitions fired or launched from land, naval and aerial platforms
into the sea such as artillery shells and aerial bombs, dumped munitions, and
shipwrecks containing unexploded ordnance.

Ordnance designed for underwater use differs from land-based ordnance in how it
is made and how it functions, particularly sea mines, torpedoes and depth
charges.

## Why this class dominates the hazard

Historical loss data shows sea mines accounted for 40 per cent of Axis vessel
losses during the North-West Europe campaign of 1944 to 1945, a larger share
than any other method of attack. The class is effective, was laid in quantity,
and persists.

## Recognition cues available at range

Manufactured geometry: regular form, smooth casing, symmetry, straight edges.
Natural seabed features and rock outcrops give irregular, discontinuous returns.
External fittings such as lugs, rings or a mooring attachment point are
manufactured-object cues.

A shipwreck containing unexploded ordnance is itself a category of contamination
here, so a wreck classification does not clear a site of ordnance risk.

Marine growth and corrosion do not reduce the hazard, and they make an object
harder to recognise rather than easier.
